A

Incident summary and facts

  • RCC has reached ground-floor level in 3 blocks.
  • Third-party 28-day cube tests: 106 of 106 footings met the required strength.
  • 23 of 56 columns failed the 28-day compressive strength test (as reported).
  • Concrete came from a local RMC supplier while the company's own batching plant was being set up.
  • The RMC contract says the RMC owner bears demolition and rebuilding cost for strength failure.
  • The RMC supplier says the problem lies in site concreting.

We are asked to list all possible causes, plan so that cube failures do not recur, and set out the RMC owner's precautions. The cause is not known; this page sets out how to find it.

B

What a 28-day cube test does and does not tell us

It measures

  • The strength of a sample of concrete, taken at a point in time
  • As affected by how that sample was taken, moulded, compacted, cured, transported and tested

It does not measure

  • The in-situ strength or capacity of the column itself
  • Compaction inside the formwork, honeycombing, cover or reinforcement
  • Who is responsible

Member can be poor while cubes pass

Poor compaction in a congested column can leave voids and honeycombing even when a separately made cube passes.

Cubes can fail while the member is adequate

A badly made or badly cured cube can fail even when the placed concrete is adequate.

For reference, IS 456:2000 (as amended) assesses compliance on groups of results: the mean of four non-overlapping consecutive results must reach at least the greater of fck + 0.825 × established standard deviation and fck + 3 N/mm², and no individual result may fall below fck − 3 N/mm². Whether "failed" in the third-party report means a group or individual non-compliance must be checked.

C

Immediate containment and evidence

Contain

  • No demolition on the strength of a cube report alone
  • Structural engineer informed at once
  • Restrict loading or access, and prop, only as the engineer advises
  • Hold similar column pours until interim controls are in place

Preserve

  • Cube reports, specimen IDs, test ages, individual values, averages, grade, lab details
  • Batch tickets, mix ID, truck no., dispatch/arrival/discharge times, slump, water or admixture additions, pump log, pour register
  • RMC batch printouts, material certificates, moisture corrections, calibration certificates, mix design and trial records
  • Site pour cards, water-addition log, sampling register, curing-tank records, demoulding and transport records

F

Investigation before demolition and the decision pathway

  1. Validate the testCheck sampling, moulding, curing, transport and lab procedure for each failed result.
  2. Rebuild traceabilityMatch each result to cube, sample, truck, batch and member.
  3. Engineer's reviewStructural engineer reviews design actions, load path and detailing of the affected columns.
  4. Further evidence as directedVisual inspection; rebound hammer and UPV as supplementary indicators only; core tests where the engineer judges them justified.
  5. Interpret cores correctlyIS 456:2000 cl. 17.4.3 treats concrete represented by cores as acceptable if the average equivalent cube strength of the cores is at least 85% of the cube strength of the grade at that age and no individual core is below 75%. This applies to cores, not to the original cube results.
  6. DecideEngineer decides: accept, test further, strengthen/repair, or demolish and rebuild.
  7. Then allocate costApply the actual contract only after the technical cause is established.

G

Quality-control plan for future pours

The aim is to reduce recurrence and achieve the specified strength consistently. No plan can guarantee that a test never fails; it can make failures rare and traceable.

Before the pour

  • Grade-specific approved mix design and trial mix; target mean strength per IS 10262 from fck and the accepted standard deviation
  • Check drawings, pour sequence, reinforcement congestion, formwork tightness, access, pump reach and vibrators
  • Agree delivery rate and truck cycle to avoid waiting and segregation
  • Approved inspection and test plan (ITP): hold points, sampling frequency, acceptance
  • Cube moulds, compaction tools, curing tank, labels and registers ready

At RMC dispatch

  • Supplier, plant, mix ID, grade, batch ticket, quantity and batch time verified
  • Calibration and material test records current
  • No unauthorised water addition; any permitted adjustment follows the approved mix and procedure, by an authorised person, recorded on the ticket
  • Delivery and placing time limits defined in the specification and enforced

At site receipt and placing

  • Check ticket and visual condition
  • Workability tests at the frequency set in the ITP; record results
  • Representative sampling per the applicable standard (IS 456 cl. 15.2.2 sets minimum sampling frequency) and the ITP
  • Place in controlled layers; avoid segregation, excessive free fall and interruptions
  • Suitable vibrators; systematic compaction without over-vibration
  • Tight formwork; truck-to-member traceability with start/end times

Cubes, curing and review

  • Clean, compliant, properly assembled moulds; prompt specimen making
  • Consistent compaction in the prescribed layers (IS 1199 Part 5)
  • Permanent unique ID linked to truck, mix, member, date and sample
  • Protected initial curing; demould and cure as prescribed; curing records
  • Chain of custody to the lab; calibrated machine; testing per IS 516
  • 7-day results as an early warning only — never final acceptance
  • Trend results by grade, supplier, truck, block, member type and date
  • Investigate every non-conforming result; close actions before the next similar pour; hold/release for critical pours

H

Precautions for the RMC owner

Plant

  • Written approved mix design; clear grade and mix ID on every ticket
  • Plant calibration, weigh-batcher checks, moisture correction, batch records kept
  • Incoming material testing; traceable source and lot records
  • Cement and admixture storage and expiry control
  • Qualified batching operators and QC staff; production per IS 4926

Delivery

  • Ticket with mix ID, batch time, quantity, water/admixture additions and truck ID
  • Transit mixer maintenance and correct drum operation
  • Delivery schedule agreed with site to minimise waiting
  • Joint slump and sampling; witnessed tests where the contract requires
  • Split / witness samples only if agreed and technically appropriate
  • Record every site addition, rejection, delay, returned load and complaint

Contract terms the RMC owner should insist on

  • Who is responsible for the mix design and for any change at site
  • Sampling, testing and acceptance procedure; independent accredited lab
  • Access to site and plant records for both parties
  • Retesting and non-conformance process; dispute resolution
  • Liability limits and exclusion of unauthorised site changes

A demolition clause does not make the supplier automatically liable. Facts first, then the actual contract.

Data limitations & assumptions

  • Only the pass/fail counts are given; individual strengths, grades, sampling records and lab reports are not available, so no cause can be confirmed.
  • We assume one result set per column; check against the lab report.
  • Standards cited (IS 456 Table 11 and cl. 15.2.2, 17.4.3; IS 10262; IS 1199 Part 5; IS 516; IS 4926) should be checked against the editions named in the project specification.
  • The full RMC contract is not available; cost allocation depends on its exact wording.

Conclusion

The 23 of 56 reported column results classified as failures (41.1%) require prompt investigation and verification against the individual laboratory reports and the applicable sample/group acceptance criteria. They do not, on their own, establish that 23 columns are unsafe, that every affected concrete batch was non-compliant, or who is at fault. The next steps are to contain, preserve evidence, rebuild batch-to-member traceability and let the structural engineer decide on further testing, acceptance, strengthening or reconstruction. Costs are then allocated under the actual contract. For future pours, a traceable QC plan — approved mixes, controlled additions, representative sampling, controlled curing and trend review — is intended to reduce recurrence. The RMC owner protects himself through calibrated batching, complete tickets, joint sampling and clear contract terms.
